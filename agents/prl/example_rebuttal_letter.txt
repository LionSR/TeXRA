Dear Dr.~Garisto,
\vspace{3em}

Thank you very much for sending out my manuscript (LK15932/Deng) for review. I appreciate the referee's time on reviewing the manuscript and his/her useful suggestions/comments, which have led me to carry out a substantial amount of new calculations and helped me to significantly improve the manuscript. Now, the current manuscript is much stronger and better than the one originally submitted to PRL. 

In his/her report, the referee A states (quote here) "My overall recommendation is to reconsider after revision". He/she has two major concerns: (1) the original manuscript did not properly demonstrate the universal applicability of the introduced RBM approach to nonlocality detections; (2) the difference between the RBM approach and the conventional DMRG approach is not clear. I take these concerns very seriously and have made the following notable efforts to address them (see my response to the referee and the updated manuscript for details): 

\begin{enumerate}
\item I carried out explicit new calculations for a 2D example with a system size beyond the state-of-the-art capability of DMRG calculations.
\item for the example of "All-to-all two-body correlators" with random measurement angles, I carried out new calculations for system sizes as large as N=60, which is inaccessible to the conventional methods (e.g., exact diagonalization).
\item I improved the presentation and emphasized the crucial differences between the RBM and DMRG approaches. 
\end{enumerate}

With these new results, I believe that I have established without any doubt the universal applicability and exceptional power of our RBM method in detecting Bell nonlocality in quantum many-body systems. I have also carefully addressed all other minor comments raised by the referee A and have made substantial changes to the manuscript accordingly. 

I stress that the interdisciplinary field of machine learning applications in quantum physics, although still very much in its infancy, has recently attracted tremendous interest across different communities. This work, which bridges machine learning and quantum many-body nonlocality for the first time, is a timely important breakthrough contribution to this fledgling field. After it was posted on arXiv, many scientists from different communities (e.g., quantum information, condensed matter physics, and quantum machine learning, etc.) have discussed with me (through emails or face-to-face conversations) about this work and possible follow-up ideas. They all think that this work is interesting, novel, inspiring, and of singular importance.  With this further improved manuscript, I believe that all the issues raised by the referee have been satisfactorily addressed and expect that it will attract even more attentions across communities. Therefore, I would like to resubmit the manuscript to PRL for your consideration. 

A detailed response to the referee A and a summary of the major revisions have been appended in the following. I have marked out the major changes in red color, so that the editor and the referee can identify them easily.

Thank you.

\vspace{3em}
Yours sincerely,
\vspace{1.5em}

Dong-Ling Deng

\subsection*{Response to Referee A}

I thank the referee for his/her careful reading of my manuscript and appreciate his/her valuable suggestions/comments, which have helped me to improve the paper significantly. I take these suggestions/comments very seriously and have done a substantial amount of new calculations accordingly. Based on his/her reports, I have also improved the presentation of the manuscript and clarified some important points. Now, the paper is notably strengthened and I believe it is suitable for PRL. The detailed response to the referee's comments is provided below.\\

\begin{referee}{A}
The manuscript by Deng examines Bell state inequalities employing a restricted Boltzmann machine to perform the calculations. The main argument for importance of this work is that it provides a novel methodology to calculate the nonlocality via violations of the Bell inequalities which is universally applicable and accurate. For me to support this for publication, I not only need to agree with that statement but also to the statement that calculating nonlocality in this fashion is actually useful. At this stage, the manuscript does not yet meet this goal. I hope under revision it will.

The main ideology of this work is that the Bell inequalities can be mapped onto the same problem as finding the ground state of effective Hamiltonians and it employs the restricted Boltzmann machine to do so. Of course, for any system that is reduced to a short-range 1d problem, this can already be done within DMRG. For other cases, it is less clear. To provide a nontrivial example, the paper also examines an all-to-all system. But it is not clear to me that such a system does not also simplify to be computationally easy as well. So I do not believe this manuscript properly demonstrates the universal application of this new technique.
\end{referee}

\begin{response}
I thank the referee for his/her nice summary of the paper and for bringing up his/her concern about the universal applicability of our RBM approach. Without any doubt, the RBM method introduced in this paper cannot solve all the problems related to quantum nonlocality detections in many-body systems, since it has been rigorously proved that the computation of the maximum violation of any multipartite Bell inequality is an NP-problem (see Ref. [42]). Unless the polynomial hierarchy (a generalization of the famous P versus NP problem) collapses in the computational complexity theory, no method can solve all these problems efficiently. Based on this, I insist that the RBM method is as universal as one may hope at best---it applies to multipartite Bell inequalities involving a polynomial number of correlators, independent of dimensionality, the order of the correlators, or whether the correlators are short-range or not. 

The referee's concern motived me to carry out additional calculations on a Bell inequality defined on a 2D honeycomb lattice (see the main text and Supplementary Material for details). In this new example, I used the RBM method to calculate the quantum violations for a system size as large as N=14\times12=168, which is inaccessible for the state-of-the-art DMRG to my knowledge. In general, DMRG is limited to 1D problems with short-range interactions (I note that there are indeed some DMRG calculations in quasi-2D, a ribbon or cylinder geometry, but the width of the ribbon or the height of the cylinder is typically limited to 10 sites to the best of my knowledge) and thus is NOT applicable for this example. I mention that one can use the RBM method to calculation the quantum violation even for a larger system size. The computational complexity scales only polynomially with the system size ~O(N^2). 

For the example with all-to-all two-body correlators (the third example in the revised manuscript), I agree with the referee that it is computationally easy if the permutation symmetry is preserved. However, in a more realistic experimental scenario, the measurement angles for each party will never be exactly the same due to various control imperfections or system noises. In this case, the permutation symmetry of the Bell inequality is not preserved for the corresponding Bell operator. This makes the computation of the quantum violation highly nontrivial. Taking into account possible experimental imperfections, in this example I consider a situation where the measurement angles are chosen randomly (hence the corresponding Bell operator does not have permutation symmetry) and show that the RBM approach works. In the revised manuscript, I have carried out new calculations for system sizes as large as N=60, which is far beyond the reach of exact diagonalization. I stress that the DMRG method is not suitable for this problem because of the all-to-all interactions. I also would like to point out that conventional Monte Carlo method is challenging for this problem as well since it apparently has a sign problem. With this new result, I believe that this example has clearly demonstrated the unparalleled power of our RBM method. 

Once again, I thank the referee for raising up this concern, which are very helpful in improving our paper. I hope that the above reply plus the newly added notable results can meet the expectations of the referee and convince him/her that the RBM method is indeed novel, powerful and relatively universal in Bell nonlocality detection, thus worth PRL publication. 
\end{response}

\begin{referee}{A}
In addition, there is an important question about how this approach compares to the DMRG approach. Can they be mapped onto each other? Is one a simplification of the other? Or are they truly different? This was not clear to me and I believe this manuscript needs a full discussion of this point.
\end{referee}

\begin{response}
The RBM approach is fundamentally distinct from DMRG for the following reasons: (1) DMRG is limited to 1D problems with short-range interactions, but RBM works for any dimension and for the case with even all-to-all interactions. This can be understood from the RBM representation of quantum many-body states, which is essentially different from the MPS (matrix product states) representation that underlies DMRG. In the RBM representation, the hidden neurons can be connected to all visible neurons (corresponding to physical qubits). As a result, the geometry of the lattice where physical qubits live, or whether the interaction is short-range or not, is in fact not important for RBM; (2) DMRG is not applicable to Bell inequalities with multipartite correlators, because there is no apparent way to write down a local MPO (matrix product operator) for this case. In contrast, the RBM approach does not have such problem, as clearly demonstrated in the last example. (3) Since MPS can only efficiently represent quantum states with small entanglement (e.g., area-law), DMRG is also limited to problems with low entanglement (see Ref. [90]). However, as extensively discussed in our previous PRX paper (Ref. [81]), entanglement is not a limiting factor for the RBM representation---RBM can represent states with massive entanglement (e.g., volume-law) very efficiently thanks to the long-range couplings between the hidden and visible neurons.  

To sum up, the RBM approach is fundamentally different from the DMRG approach and they cannot be mapped onto each other. The referee's comment on this point reflects that I probably did not explain sufficiently clear the differences between the two approaches in the previous version. In the revised manuscript, I have improved the presentation and emphasized their differences more explicitly.
\end{response}

\begin{referee}{A}
(1) The concept of nonlocality, namely finding quantum states that violate Bell inequalities, does not appear to me to be a mainstream topic. I am not clear on what it is used for aside from the Bell inequalities. It also strikes me as being closely related to entanglement. In order to make a case that this is an important quantity to measure, the manuscript needs to clearly discuss the distinction between these two concepts and explain why nonlocality is important.
\end{referee}

\begin{response}
I thank the referee for raising this important point, which helps me improve the presentation. Entanglement and nonlocality are two closely related but fundamentally distinct concepts: any quantum state that manifests nonlocality is necessarily entangled, but the opposite is NOT true---there exist entangled states that admit a local-hidden-variable description (hence they do not violate any Bell inequality and cannot show nonlocality properties). In this sense, nonlocality is a stronger quantum property and detection of nonlocality is a sufficient condition to demonstrate entanglement. In fact, many experiments have indeed used nonlocality to show observations of entanglement of the systems. From a practical point of view, nonlocality has been shown to be an indispensable resource for a number of device-independent (DI) technologies, such as DI quantum key distribution, DI randomness expansion or amplification, and DI self-testing, etc. For these applications, demonstration of entanglement is not enough. I refer to the Rev. Mod. Phys. paper (Ref. [3]) for a more comprehensive explanation of the difference between entanglement and nonlocality and of the practical applications of nonlocality. 

In the revised manuscript, I have clearly discussed the difference between entanglement and nonlocality, and have emphasized the importance of nonlocality in practical applications. 
\end{response}

\begin{referee}{A}
(2) The construction of the RBM appears to me to be close to the construction of matrix product states for DMRG. A clear distinction of how these two methods are different (or are they actually the same) is important. For, this technique might have much broader applicability if it can properly find energies and wavefunctions of Hamiltonians in higher dimensions and is as accurate as DMRG in one-d. This would make the technique much more important than even this paper implies it is, as it could be applied to a wide class of problems. If, on the other hand it is something that is less accurate than DMRG and does not have wide applicability, it becomes less interesting.
\end{referee}

\begin{response}
The RBM approach is fundamentally different from the DMRG approach. I have discussed this in the above reply. As for the connection between the RBM and MPS representations, we have the following known results (see a detailed discussion in Ref. [81,69]): (1) RBMs can represent some quantum states with massive entanglement (e.g., volume-law) very efficiently. These states do not have efficient MPS representation; (2) All short-range RBM states satisfy an entanglement area law and can be efficiently represented by MPS. Whether all MPS with small bond dimension can be efficiently represented by RBMs or not is an interesting and important open question in the literature. 

The referee is correct that the RBM method may have much broader applicability if it is truly different from DMRG. In fact, in one of our previous work (Ref. [48]), we have used the RBM method to study topological phases and phase transitions. I mention that using RBM to tackle quantum many-body problems is a new fledgling direction, very much in its infancy. A lot of important questions and exciting possibilities remain unexplored. But they are beyond the scope of the current paper, and I would like to leave them for future work.

In the revised manuscript, I have added a new 2D example to clearly show that the RBM approach in detecting nonlocality is not limited to 1D. 
\end{response}

\begin{referee}{A}
(3) It appears to me that the main strategy for this work is to take a Bell inequality, convert its violation into a minimization problem for an effective Hamiltonian and then use the RBM to solve that problem approximately. If this is what is being done, it should be stated clearly in this fashion. Currently the manuscript is not so clear on precisely how this proceeds.
\end{referee}

\begin{response}
I appreciate this insightful suggestion. In the revised manuscript, I followed this suggestion and added "Alternatively, one can think about the problem in another way: for a fixed measurement setting, the corresponding Bell operator reduces to an effective Hamiltonian and finding out the maximal Bell violation is then reduced to finding out the ground state energy of this Hamiltonian, which can be done with RBM-based reinforcement learning." to make it more accessible to the general audience.
\end{response}

\begin{referee}{A}
(4) It is probably useful in the supplemental information to indicate how Eq. 1 can be converted into the conventional form of a sum over product states with arbitrary coefficients.
\end{referee}

\begin{response}
I thank the referee for this helpful suggestion. In this resubmitted version, I have made this clear by adding into the main text the following two sentences: "It is worthwhile to clarify that the RBM state defined above is a variational state, with \Phi_M\left(\Xi,\Omega\right) specifying the complex coefficient for each component. The actual quantum state should be understood as |\left.\Psi\left(\Omega\right)\right\rangle=\sum_{\Xi}{\Phi_M\left(\Xi,\Omega\right)}|\left.\Xi\right\rangle (up to an irrelevant normalization constant).
\end{response}

\begin{referee}{A}
(5) The paper talks about performing minimization, but is less clear on whether this is done to find an approximate minimum or it finds the exact minimum. I strongly suspect it is the former and the paper needs to state this clearly.
\end{referee}

\begin{response}
I agree with the referee that with fixed number of hidden neurons, the RBM method can only find an approximate minimum to certain precision. But this precision can be systematically improved by increasing the number of hidden neurons or iterations in the training process. In fact, one can obtain arbitrary accuracy in principle due to the universal representability theorem (any quantum state can be approximated to arbitrary accuracy by the RBM representation, as long as the number of hidden neurons is large enough). 

I also want to clarify that the RBM approach may in principle converge to some local minima. This is a common issue in the machine learning literature. For the examples considered in this paper, our RBM approach seems always find the global minimum. In the revised manuscript, I have clarified these important points. 
\end{response}

\begin{referee}{A}
(6) For the all-to-all problem, does it map onto something similar to the Lipkin model? Is there an analog of total spin that is a symmetry and greatly simplifies the problem?
\end{referee}

\begin{response}
I thank the referee for making this insightful point. Yes, if all the parties choose exactly the same measurement settings, then the permutation symmetry of the Bell inequality will be preserved and the problem is reduced to something similar to the Lipkin model and is computationally easy. However, as explained in the above reply, if we consider a more realistic experimental scenario where imperfections or noises are included and the measurement angles are not exactly the same for each party, then the permutation symmetry breaks and the problem is highly nontrivial. In the revised manuscript, I have carried out additional calculations for this nontrivial case for a system size as large as N=60, which is inaccessible for traditional methods such as exact diagonalization. 
\end{response}

\begin{referee}{A}
(7) Normally and RBM has just one hidden layer. Is that the case here?
\end{referee}

\begin{response}
Yes, the referee is correct. The RBM used here also has just one hidden layer, similar to the one used in classical machine learning. But, unlike the RBM used in classical machine learning, the internal parameters for the RBM used in this paper are complex numbers. We use complex parameters in order to provide a complete description of both the amplitude and the phase of the quantum state. 
\end{response}

\begin{referee}{A}
(8) In the SI, when we compare to the exact result, is that found from exact diagonalization?
\end{referee}

\begin{response}
Yes, the exact results are obtained from exact diagonalization. I have clarified this point in the revised SI. 
\end{response}

In summary, I thank the referee for his/her valuable suggestions/comments.  Following these comments and suggestions, I have done substantial additional calculations and added a completely new 2D example to demonstrate the unparalleled power of our RBM approach in detecting Bell nonlocality. In addition, I have clarified all the confusing points and improved the presentation. I hope this significantly enhanced manuscript will satisfy the referee and convince him/her to recommend publication of this work in PRL.
