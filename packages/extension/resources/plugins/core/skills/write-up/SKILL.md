# Writing work up for a reader

Solving and writing are different jobs. While you work, work however you like. This skill is for afterwards: the reader was not there, and a person can only trust what they can follow.

You cannot judge that yourself. You know what every symbol means and which steps were hard, so your own text always reads as clear to you. A reader who saw none of the work can judge it, and `agent` gives you one: a child sees none of your conversation.

## Explain it to a fresh agent first

Write an explanation addressed to someone who has seen nothing: what was asked, what is now known, and why it is true. Then start a child with that explanation and the files it needs, and nothing else. Two ways to use it, and you may use both:

- **As the reader.** Ask it to say back, in its own words, what is claimed and how the argument goes, and to list every place it had to guess, could not follow, or could not check. What it says back wrongly is what your text actually says.
- **As the writer.** Ask it to write the note for the human reader from your explanation, and to report what it could not justify from what you gave it. It writes without your working vocabulary and without the history, because it never had them.

Its questions are findings. Some are fixed by a clearer sentence. Others show that a step is missing; fix those by doing the mathematics, never by wording around them. After a substantial revision use a new child: one that read the earlier version is no longer a fresh reader.

## What the reader needs

- **What is known, and how firmly.** State each result exactly, with its hypotheses, and say which kind it is: proved, proved under a stated assumption, supported by computation or examples, conjectured, or tried and failed. Never let a weaker kind read as a stronger one.
- **A map before the details.** A short account a reader can stop after: the objects, the main steps with their precise statements, and the idea behind each. Every sentence of it literally true.
- **The structure of the argument in the text.** Label each statement and let each proof name the earlier results it uses. A reader should be able to check one step knowing only the statements it depends on, and to see which steps everything else rests on.
- **Where to look hardest.** Say which steps you are least sure of and why. A reader's attention is the scarce thing; point it.
- **Gaps left visible.** A step you could not justify stays marked as one.

Leave out how the work went: the order you tried things in, the names you gave things along the way, and attempts that failed, except where knowing one saves the reader from repeating it.

## When the work is a direction, not one result

A direction rarely ends in one theorem. Write it as a map of the territory: the questions, what is settled about each and how firmly, how the results depend on one another, which approaches are exhausted and why, and what the most promising open question is. The same test applies: a fresh agent given only the note should be able to say where the direction stands and pick up the next question without asking you anything.
